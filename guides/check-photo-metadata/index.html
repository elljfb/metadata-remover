Photo metadata guide

How to find metadata on a photo

To find photo metadata, open the image’s information or properties panel on your device. It may show EXIF details such as capture time, camera model, settings, or GPS location. You can start with the built-in tools below; they are convenient, but they do not all display the same fields or read every metadata format.

Check the actual image file you intend to share. A photo in a cloud library, an edited export, a screenshot, or a copy downloaded from a social app may not have the same metadata as the original camera file. If you are checking privacy, inspect the final copy—not just the original on your device.

Check photo metadata on Windows

  • In File Explorer, right-click the photo and choose Properties.
  • Open the Details tab to review fields Windows can read, such as dimensions, camera information, and capture date.
  • Look for camera, date, and location entries in the list. Not every field is shown for every format, and the tab may not expose XMP or other less common metadata.

If the image is open in another Windows app, its Info panel may show a different selection of details. Check the original file’s Properties rather than relying only on a thumbnail or preview.

View photo information on a Mac

  • Open the image in Preview.
  • Choose Tools → Show Inspector (or press Command-I), then review the information tabs for image dimensions and other details.
  • If the file includes location information, look for a GPS or location view in the inspector. Its presence and layout depend on the file and macOS version.

Photos and other library apps can show details about the library item rather than every field embedded in the underlying file. If a field is important, inspect an exported copy of the exact format you plan to share.

Find photo details on iPhone or iPad

  • Open the image in the Photos app.
  • Tap the Info button or swipe up. Photos may show the date, device or camera details, and a map when location information is available.
  • If there is no map or location entry, the app may not have a GPS value to show, or the particular copy may not include it.

Check metadata on Android

  • Open the image in your Gallery or Google Photos app.
  • Open the photo’s Info or Details view (in Google Photos, swipe up) and look for the file details and location section.
  • Menu names and the metadata displayed differ across Android devices and gallery apps. If you have more than one copy, confirm you are looking at the downloaded file you intend to share.

What do common photo metadata fields mean?

  • Date and time: a camera’s capture time can differ from a file’s created or modified date. Some files do not include a time-zone offset, so treat the displayed time as a clue rather than a guaranteed local time.
  • GPS or location: coordinates may be shown as numbers or on a map. They refer to the recorded camera location and may be absent, imprecise, or removed from a copy.
  • Camera and lens: make, model, lens, and exposure settings describe capture equipment and conditions; these are not proof of who took the picture.
  • Software, author, and copyright: these fields may be added or changed by editing or catalog software and can be blank or out of date.
  • Dimensions and orientation: these describe image size and how some software should display it; they do not tell you whether GPS or other personal metadata is present.

Can built-in tools show every EXIF field?

No. Built-in viewers can omit fields, and photo metadata is broader than EXIF alone. A properties panel that shows no GPS information does not prove that every kind of metadata has been removed. The image may have no GPS tag, the app may hide it, or the copy you are inspecting may differ from the original.

For a more detailed inspection, use a reputable desktop metadata utility such as ExifTool. If you already have it installed, the read-only command exiftool -a -G1 -s photo.jpg lists recognized fields with their groups. Replace photo.jpg with the path to your image. Review the output locally for a sensitive photo, and avoid sending private images to an unfamiliar online metadata checker.
